SLLE: Saudi Laboratory Licensure Exam guide

Last checked against official documents: 17 November 2026

The SLLE is the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) licensing exam for laboratory specialists. It has 200 multiple-choice questions in two parts of 100, with 120 minutes for each part, and each question has four options with one best answer. The pass mark is a scaled score of 530 on a 200–800 scale. Blood Bank, Clinical Chemistry, Hematology and Microbiology each carry 15–20% of the exam. Up to 10% of questions are unscored pilot items.

Question 1

A Rh(D)-negative woman at 20 weeks of gestation in her second pregnancy has a positive antibody screen, and the antibody is identified as anti-D. The obstetrician asks what the laboratory should do next to follow the risk to the fetus. What is the most appropriate test?

  1. Kleihauer-Betke test on the maternal blood
  2. Antibody titration of the maternal plasma
  3. Direct antiglobulin test on the mother's red cells
  4. Issue a dose of Rh(D) immune globulin
Show answer

B. Antibody titration of the maternal plasma Once a clinically significant antibody such as anti-D is identified in a pregnant woman, the antibody is titrated and repeated at intervals; a rising titre above the critical level prompts fetal assessment. A Kleihauer-Betke test measures fetal cells in the mother after a bleed. The mother's DAT is expected to be negative, because her antibody targets fetal cells. Rh immune globulin does not help once she is already sensitised.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Blood Collection (Pretransfusion Testing)

Question 2

A Rh(D)-negative woman with no anti-D delivers a Rh(D)-positive baby. Within what time after delivery should Rh(D) immune globulin be given?

  1. 14 days
  2. 7 days
  3. 72 hours
  4. Before her next pregnancy
Show answer

C. 72 hours Rh(D) immune globulin is given within 72 hours of delivery of a D-positive baby, or of any event that may cause fetomaternal bleeding, to clear fetal D-positive cells before the mother's immune system becomes sensitised. Giving it later than this, or waiting until the next pregnancy, is outside the recommended window and the protective effect may be lost.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate

Question 3

A pregnant woman reports leaking fluid. Vaginal fluid turns Nitrazine paper blue. What does this suggest?

  1. Bacterial vaginosis rather than rupture
  2. Acidic fluid, consistent with normal urine
  3. Alkaline fluid, likely amniotic fluid
  4. Normal acidic vaginal secretions only
Show answer

C. Alkaline fluid, likely amniotic fluid Normal vaginal fluid is acidic, while amniotic fluid is alkaline and turns Nitrazine paper blue, supporting rupture of membranes. Ferning of dried fluid on a slide is another indicator. A blue result is not proof, because blood, semen, alkaline antiseptics, urine or bacterial vaginosis can also raise the pH.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Prelabor Rupture of Membranes (PROM)

Question 4

A 72-year-old man with bone pain has alkaline phosphatase three times the upper limit. His GGT and bilirubin are normal. What is the most likely source of the raised alkaline phosphatase?

  1. Obstructed bile ducts
  2. Bone
  3. Alcohol-related liver injury
  4. Hepatocellular damage from hepatitis
Show answer

B. Bone Alkaline phosphatase comes mainly from liver and bone. GGT rises with liver and bile-duct disease but not with bone disease, so a high ALP with a normal GGT points to bone (for example Paget disease or metastases). Biliary obstruction and alcohol-related liver injury would both raise GGT. Hepatitis mainly raises ALT and AST rather than ALP alone.

Source: Testing.com (ADLM) – Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT)

Question 5

A patient with clear symptoms of hyperthyroidism has a suppressed TSH, but free T4 is within the reference range. Which test should be done next?

  1. Serum free or total T3
  2. Repeat TSH in 6 months
  3. Anti-thyroglobulin antibody
  4. Serum calcitonin
Show answer

A. Serum free or total T3 When TSH is suppressed but free T4 is normal in a symptomatic patient, T3 should be measured to detect T3 toxicosis, where only T3 is raised (seen in early Graves disease or toxic nodules). If T3 is also normal, the pattern is subclinical hyperthyroidism. Simply waiting 6 months misses treatable disease. Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies support autoimmune thyroiditis but do not explain the symptoms. Calcitonin is a medullary thyroid cancer marker.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Hyperthyroidism

Question 6

A patient with massive splenomegaly has a blood film with many teardrop-shaped red cells, nucleated red cells and myelocytes. Which condition is most likely?

  1. Vitamin B12 deficiency
  2. Iron deficiency anaemia
  3. Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
  4. Primary myelofibrosis
Show answer

D. Primary myelofibrosis Teardrop cells (dacrocytes) with a leukoerythroblastic picture (nucleated red cells and immature granulocytes) and massive splenomegaly are typical of primary myelofibrosis, where marrow fibrosis forces blood formation into the spleen and liver. Iron deficiency shows small pale cells and pencil cells. Autoimmune haemolysis shows spherocytes. B12 deficiency shows macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Primary Myelofibrosis

Question 7

A patient's red cell indices show an MCV of 68 fL. How is this anaemia classified?

  1. Macrocytic
  2. Normocytic
  3. Microcytic
  4. Megaloblastic
Show answer

C. Microcytic Red cells are called microcytic when the MCV is below about 80 fL and macrocytic when it is above 100 fL; 68 fL is microcytic. The main causes are iron deficiency, thalassaemia, anaemia of chronic disease (later stages) and sideroblastic anaemia. Normocytic means an MCV of about 80–100 fL. Megaloblastic anaemia is a type of macrocytic anaemia caused by B12 or folate deficiency.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Evaluation of Anemia

Question 8

A 19-year-old student has fever, exudative pharyngitis, posterior cervical lymph node enlargement and atypical lymphocytes on the blood film. Which rapid test is commonly used to screen for the likely cause?

  1. Antistreptolysin O (ASO) titre
  2. VDRL flocculation test for reagin
  3. Widal tube agglutination test
  4. Heterophile antibody (monospot) test
Show answer

D. Heterophile antibody (monospot) test The picture suggests Epstein-Barr virus infectious mononucleosis, and the heterophile antibody (monospot) card agglutination test is the usual rapid screen. ASO shows a recent streptococcal infection and does not explain atypical lymphocytes. The Widal test is an old serological test for typhoid. VDRL is a non-treponemal screening test for syphilis.

Source: MSD Manual Professional – Infectious Mononucleosis

Question 9

A nursing student has HBsAg negative, total anti-HBc negative and anti-HBs positive. What is the most likely interpretation?

  1. Acute hepatitis B infection
  2. Resolved past hepatitis B infection
  3. Immune from hepatitis B vaccination
  4. Chronic hepatitis B infection
Show answer

C. Immune from hepatitis B vaccination Hepatitis B vaccine contains only HBsAg, so a vaccinated person makes anti-HBs but never anti-HBc. Isolated anti-HBs therefore indicates immunity from vaccination. Resolved natural infection shows both anti-HBc and anti-HBs. Acute infection shows HBsAg with IgM anti-HBc. Chronic infection shows HBsAg persisting for more than 6 months with total anti-HBc, while anti-HBs is negative.

Source: CDC – Hepatitis B: Clinical Testing and Diagnosis

Question 10

A liver biopsy from a patient with suspected haemochromatosis needs a stain to show ferric iron (haemosiderin). Which stain is used?

  1. Congo red
  2. Masson trichrome
  3. Ziehl-Neelsen
  4. Perls Prussian blue
Show answer

D. Perls Prussian blue Perls Prussian blue reaction uses potassium ferrocyanide in acid; it reacts with ferric (Fe³⁺) iron to form blue ferric ferrocyanide, showing haemosiderin in tissue, blood or marrow smears. It is used for iron overload and ring sideroblasts. Congo red stains amyloid. Ziehl-Neelsen stains acid-fast bacilli. Masson trichrome stains collagen and is used to grade fibrosis.

Source: Leica Biosystems – An Introduction to Routine and Special Staining

Question 11

A stool isolate from a child with bloody diarrhoea is a non-lactose fermenter. On TSI it gives an alkaline slant, an acid butt, no gas and no H2S, and it is non-motile. Which organism is most likely?

  1. Salmonella Typhimurium
  2. Shigella species
  3. Proteus mirabilis
  4. Escherichia coli
Show answer

B. Shigella species Shigella typically gives K/A with little or no gas and no H2S on TSI, is non-motile and does not ferment lactose, and it causes bloody diarrhoea (dysentery). Most Salmonella produce H2S and are motile. Proteus produces H2S, swarms and is strongly urease-positive. E. coli usually ferments lactose (acid slant and butt with gas) and is motile.

Source: WHO/CDC – Manual for the Laboratory Identification and AST of Bacterial Pathogens (2003)

Question 12

Rice-water stool from a patient with severe watery diarrhoea is cultured on TCBS agar. What colony appearance suggests Vibrio cholerae?

  1. Black-centred colonies
  2. Yellow colonies
  3. Green colonies
  4. Pink colonies with a bile precipitate
Show answer

B. Yellow colonies V. cholerae ferments sucrose in TCBS agar, and the acid turns the bromothymol blue indicator yellow, giving yellow, shiny colonies 2–4 mm across. Green colonies are sucrose non-fermenters such as V. parahaemolyticus. Black-centred colonies are typical of H₂S producers such as Salmonella on XLD or SS agar. Pink colonies with bile precipitate describe lactose fermenters such as E. coli on MacConkey agar.

Source: WHO/CDC – Manual for the Laboratory Identification and AST of Bacterial Pathogens (2003)

Question 13

On a Levey-Jennings chart, the last 10 consecutive glucose control values are all above the mean, though each is within ±2 SD. Which Westgard rule is violated?

  1. 1-2s
  2. R-4s
  3. No rule; the run is in control
  4. 10x
Show answer

D. 10x The 10x rule rejects when 10 consecutive control values fall on the same side of the mean, which signals a shift such as a new reagent lot or a calibration change, even though no single value is outside 2 SD. The 1-2s rule needs one value beyond 2 SD. R-4s needs one control above +2 SD and another below −2 SD in the same run. Calling the run in control would ignore the shift.

Source: Westgard QC – Westgard Rules and Multirule QC

Question 14

A private laboratory patient is surprised by a large bill for an extended test panel that no one explained beforehand. Under the Saudi Code of Ethics for Healthcare Practitioners, what should have happened?

  1. Fees should be discussed only if the patient complains
  2. Fees may be set after results are known
  3. The doctor alone decides, without informing the patient
  4. Estimated fees should be told beforehand
Show answer

D. Estimated fees should be told beforehand The SCFHS Code requires that patients be informed about the estimated fees for care before the service is provided, and that fees be fair and not exaggerated. This lets patients make informed decisions about optional tests. Setting fees after results, discussing them only after complaints, or deciding without informing the patient all breach this duty.

Source: SCFHS – Code of Ethics for Healthcare Practitioners (English)

Question 15

Under the Saudi Code of Ethics for Healthcare Practitioners, who owns the contents of a patient's medical record in a hospital?

  1. The health institution
  2. The treating doctor personally
  3. The laboratory staff
  4. The insurance company
Show answer

A. The health institution The SCFHS Code states that medical record contents are the property of the institution. Practitioners must keep clear and accurate records and protect their confidentiality, and patients have the right to information and copies under the rules. Individual doctors, laboratory staff and insurers do not own the record.

Source: SCFHS – Code of Ethics for Healthcare Practitioners (English)

Frequently asked questions

How many questions are in the SLLE, and how long is it?

200 multiple-choice questions, in 2 parts of 100; four options, one best answer. Time: 120 minutes for each part, with a 30-minute break between them.

What is the SLLE pass mark?

The pass mark is a scaled score of 530 on a 200–800 scale, per the SCFHS SLLE guide. It is not a simple percentage of correct answers.

What topics does the SLLE cover?

Nine domains: Blood Bank, Clinical Chemistry, Hematology and Microbiology (15–20% each), and Urinalysis and Body Fluids, Immunology and Serology, Histo- and Cyto-Techniques, Laboratory Operations, and Patient Safety and Professionalism (5–10% each).

Are all SLLE questions scored?

No. Up to 10% of the questions are unscored pilot items, but you can't tell which ones, so answer every question carefully.
