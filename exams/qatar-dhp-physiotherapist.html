Qatar physiotherapy exam (DHP Physiotherapist) guide

Last checked against official documents: 17 November 2026

Qatar's Physiotherapist qualifying exam is run by the Department of Healthcare Professions (DHP, formerly QCHP) through Prometric. It has 150 multiple-choice questions in 3 hours, and the pass mark is 50%. You can take it an unlimited number of times. The blueprint has the same 10 domains and weights as the Saudi SCFHS physiotherapy exam: 18 questions each for Anatomy and Physiology, Special Populations, Neurorehabilitation and Musculoskeletal Rehabilitation.

Try 15 practice questions

Written by Ijtiaz Med for practice, from the app's free starter set. These are not questions from the real exam.

Question 1

What is the main action of the piriformis muscle at the hip?

  1. Flexion of the hip joint
  2. Extension of the knee joint
  3. Adduction of the thigh
  4. Lateral rotation of the thigh
Show answer

D. Lateral rotation of the thigh The piriformis runs from the anterior sacrum through the greater sciatic notch to the greater trochanter. It is one of the deep lateral rotators of the hip, turning the thigh outward, and it also stabilises the hip joint. The sciatic nerve usually passes just below it, so a tight or irritated piriformis can cause buttock pain with sciatic symptoms. Hip flexion is mainly by iliopsoas, adduction by the adductor group and knee extension by the quadriceps.

Source: OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology (CC BY) 11.6 Appendicular Muscles of the Pelvic Girdle and Lower Limbs

Question 2

During open-chain knee extension (sitting, extending the leg), in which direction does the tibia glide on the femur?

  1. It does not glide, it only spins
  2. Posteriorly, opposite to the movement
  3. Medially toward the other knee
  4. Anteriorly, with the movement
Show answer

D. Anteriorly, with the movement By the convex-concave rule, when a concave joint surface moves on a fixed convex surface, it rolls and glides in the same direction as the bone moves. In open-chain knee extension the concave tibial plateau moves on the convex femoral condyles, so the tibia rolls and glides anteriorly, and the tibia rotates externally at the end of extension (screw-home). In closed-chain extension the convex femur moves on the tibia, so the femur glides posteriorly.

Source: University of Oklahoma HSC (D. Thompson) – Biomechanics of the Knee

Question 3

During a spinal cord injury motor examination, the therapist tests the ankle plantar flexors. Which key myotome do they represent?

  1. S1
  2. S3
  3. L5
  4. L4
Show answer

A. S1 In the International Standards, the ankle plantar flexors (gastrocnemius and soleus) are the key muscle for S1. The ankle dorsiflexors represent L4 and the long toe extensors L5. S3 has no key muscle on the standard motor examination. The S1 root also carries the Achilles reflex, so S1 involvement may weaken plantar flexion and reduce the ankle jerk.

Source: International Standards for Neurological Classification of SCI (ISNCSCI) worksheet – NINDS Common Data Elements

Question 4

During an antenatal exercise class, a woman at 28 weeks of pregnancy reports a new, painful, swollen and warm right calf. What should the therapist do?

  1. Apply a hot pack and review next week
  2. Massage the calf to ease the swelling
  3. Continue with lighter leg exercises
  4. Stop and arrange urgent medical review
Show answer

D. Stop and arrange urgent medical review Pregnancy increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis, and a swollen, hot, painful calf is a warning sign to stop exercise. The therapist should stop the session and arrange urgent medical assessment, because a clot can travel to the lungs (pulmonary embolism). Massaging the calf, continuing exercise or applying heat are inappropriate until DVT has been ruled out. Other stop signs include bleeding, fluid leakage, chest pain and reduced fetal movement.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Pregnancy-Related Conditions (PTA 104)

Question 5

A current with a very slow rise time fails to produce a muscle contraction, although the same peak intensity with a fast rise does. What explains this?

  1. The electrodes are too far apart
  2. The current is too high in frequency
  3. The muscle has become denervated
  4. Nerve accommodation to the slow rise
Show answer

D. Nerve accommodation to the slow rise Accommodation is the reduced excitability of nerve membranes when a stimulus rises slowly: the membrane adapts as the current increases, so it may not reach threshold even at the same peak intensity. A faster rise time produces a response. If the muscle were denervated, it would not respond to the fast-rising pulse either, electrode spacing affects depth and current spread rather than rise time, and frequency is not what differs between the two stimuli.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Foundations of Electrical Stimulation (PTA 101)

Question 6

Which massage stroke is a pressure technique that kneads and squeezes the muscle?

  1. Effleurage
  2. Tapotement
  3. Petrissage
  4. Vibration
Show answer

C. Petrissage Massage strokes are grouped by their action. Effleurage is a stroking technique, usually centripetal toward the heart; petrissage is a pressure technique of kneading, wringing and squeezing the muscle to mobilise tissue; and tapotement is a percussion technique such as rapid clapping or hacking, used for a stimulating effect. Vibration is a rapid shaking motion. Petrissage is therefore the kneading, pressure stroke.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Manual Therapy (PTA 101)

Question 7

A 16-year-old swimmer is cleared to resume training after a shoulder injury. How should training be resumed?

  1. Train only through pain to test the shoulder
  2. Return straight to the previous full load
  3. Double the volume to regain fitness quickly
  4. Gradually build back up to the pre-injury level
Show answer

D. Gradually build back up to the pre-injury level After injury, athletes should resume gradually, building training volume and intensity back up toward the pre-injury level while monitoring symptoms, and checking with their doctor or therapist before new activities. Returning straight to full load or doubling volume to catch up overloads tissues that have deconditioned and risks re-injury, and training through pain ignores a warning sign.

Source: KidsHealth (Nemours): Sports Injuries (for teens)

Question 8

Two patients of the same age follow an identical strengthening programme, but one gains strength much faster than the other. Which training principle explains this?

  1. Reversibility
  2. Specificity
  3. Individual differences
  4. Diminishing returns
Show answer

C. Individual differences The principle of individual differences recognises that people respond differently to the same training stimulus because of genetics, initial fitness, age, sleep, nutrition, health and motivation, so programmes must be individualised and progressed according to each person's response. Specificity relates adaptations to the type of training, reversibility to loss of fitness when training stops, and diminishing returns to smaller gains as fitness improves.

Source: LibreTexts: Concepts of Fitness (Arkansas State University–Beebe) 1.3 Principles of Adaptation to Stress

Question 9

Which type of cerebral palsy is the most common?

  1. Hypotonic
  2. Spastic
  3. Dyskinetic
  4. Ataxic
Show answer

B. Spastic Spastic cerebral palsy, characterised by increased muscle tone and stiffness from upper motor neuron damage, is by far the most common type, affecting about 80% of people with CP; it is described by distribution as hemiplegia, diplegia (legs more than arms) or quadriplegia. Dyskinetic CP involves uncontrolled writhing or jerky movements, ataxic CP affects balance and coordination, and hypotonia alone is a less common presentation.

Source: CDC: About Cerebral Palsy, 2024

Question 10

A 12-year-old is prescribed a rigid brace for idiopathic scoliosis. How many hours a day is full-time bracing usually effective?

  1. Only during sport
  2. Only on weekends
  3. 18 to 23 hours a day
  4. 1 to 2 hours a day
Show answer

C. 18 to 23 hours a day Rigid scoliosis orthoses work by applying corrective forces during growth, and effective full-time wear ranges from about 18 to 23 hours a day, with some protocols using night-time braces. Adolescents often find braces uncomfortable or embarrassing and are more likely to avoid wearing them, so education, regular skin checks and encouragement are an important part of physiotherapy. Wearing the brace for only a short time each day or only during sport is not effective.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Spinal Orthoses (PTA 104)

Question 11

A worker collapses without a pulse after an electrocution accident. Which rhythm is most likely, in which the ventricles quiver and pump no blood?

  1. Atrial flutter
  2. Sinus bradycardia
  3. Ventricular fibrillation
  4. First-degree heart block
Show answer

C. Ventricular fibrillation Ventricular fibrillation is uncontrolled quivering of the ventricular muscle, so no blood is pumped and the patient is pulseless. Causes include heart attack, electrocution, injury to the heart and severe potassium disturbance. It needs immediate CPR and defibrillation. Sinus bradycardia and first-degree heart block still produce a pulse, and atrial flutter affects the atria while the ventricles keep pumping.

Source: MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia: Ventricular fibrillation

Question 12

A 22-year-old swimmer has shoulder pain with overhead activity. Using Neer's stages, what characterises stage I impingement?

  1. Cuff fibrosis, usually between 25 and 40
  2. Bony spurs with complete cuff rupture
  3. Rotator cuff tearing with chronic pain
  4. Oedema and haemorrhage, age 25 or under
Show answer

D. Oedema and haemorrhage, age 25 or under Neer's stages of subacromial impingement are based on age, pain and the degree of rotator cuff involvement. Stage I involves oedema and haemorrhage in the subacromial space, typically in people aged 25 or less, and is reversible with rest and conservative care. Stage II involves fibrosis and tendinitis of the cuff, biceps or bursa, typically at ages 25 to 40, and stage III involves rotator cuff tears and bony changes with chronic pain.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Shoulder (PTA 104)

Question 13

An athlete wants to return to full sport 8 weeks after a significant ligament injury because it no longer hurts. Roughly how strong is scar tissue about 60 days after injury compared with the original tissue?

  1. Exactly 100% as strong
  2. Less than 5% as strong
  3. About 70–80% as strong
  4. Stronger than the original tissue
Show answer

C. About 70–80% as strong Healing tissue goes through inflammation, proliferation and remodelling, and remodelling can continue for many months. About 60 days after injury, scar tissue is typically only 70 to 80 percent as strong as the original tissue, even if pain has settled. This is why loading is progressed gradually and return to sport is based on functional criteria rather than absence of pain alone. Scar tissue is not stronger than the original, nor almost absent at this stage.

Source: Lane Community College PTA course notes: Tissue Response to Injury (PTA 101)

Question 14

A 15-year-old boy with left Bell's palsy cannot fully close his left eye. What advice should the therapist give to protect the eye, especially at night?

  1. Keep the eye open to the air to strengthen the eyelid
  2. Perform forceful blinking exercises every hour overnight
  3. Apply a warm compress to the closed eye for 30 minutes
  4. Use lubricating eye drops and an eye patch while sleeping
Show answer

D. Use lubricating eye drops and an eye patch while sleeping When the eyelid cannot close fully, the cornea can dry out and be injured. Lubricating eye drops and an eye patch or shield keep the eye moist and protected, especially during sleep. A warm compress does not prevent drying. Leaving the eye exposed risks corneal damage. Forceful blinking all night is not possible during sleep and does not protect the eye.

Source: NINDS: Bell's Palsy, 2024

Question 15

A carpet fitter who kneels most of the day has a soft, fluid-filled swelling directly over the front of his kneecap. Knee movement is near normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. Popliteal (Baker) cyst
  2. Prepatellar bursitis
  3. Meniscal cyst
  4. Patellar tendinopathy
Show answer

B. Prepatellar bursitis Bursitis is swelling and irritation of a bursa, often from overuse or repeated pressure. Prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee) follows prolonged kneeling and produces a soft swelling over the front of the patella. Patellar tendinopathy causes pain below the kneecap with jumping. A Baker cyst lies behind the knee, and a meniscal cyst sits at the joint line. Redness, heat and fever would raise concern for an infected bursa.

Source: MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia: Bursitis
